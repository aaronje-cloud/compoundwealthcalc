How much money do I need to retire early?

Updated October 30, 2026 · 6 min read · By Aaron Eason

Here's the short answer: most people need about 25 times what they spend in a year. If you spend $40,000 a year, you need about $1 million invested.

Key points

  • Your number depends on what you spend, not what you earn.
  • The 4% rule says you can take out about 4% in your first year of retirement.
  • If you retire very early, aiming for 28 to 30 times your spending is safer.

Step 1: Figure out what you spend

Add up a normal year of spending. Include rent or mortgage, food, car, insurance, phone, fun and travel. Don't forget health insurance. If you retire before 65, you'll need to pay for it yourself.

Think about what changes when you retire. Maybe your home is paid off. Maybe you'll travel more.

Step 2: Multiply by 25

This comes from the 4% rule. Researchers studied past U.S. markets. They found a mix of stocks and bonds usually lasted at least 30 years when you took out 4% the first year, then raised it a little each year for rising prices.

4% is the same as 1 out of 25. So 25 times your spending is your target. We call it your freedom number.

Step 3: Adjust for retiring early

The 4% rule was tested for 30-year retirements. If you retire at 40, your money may need to last 50 years. That's why many early retirees use 3.5% instead. It means saving about 28.5 times your spending.

Step 4: Subtract other income

Will you have Social Security, a pension, rental income or a part-time job? Those lower how much your investments need to cover.

Say you spend $50,000 a year. A part-time job pays $20,000. Now your investments only need to cover $30,000. Your number drops from $1.25 million to $750,000.

Things the 4% rule doesn't cover

  • Taxes. Money from a traditional 401(k) is taxed. Plan for it.
  • Early withdrawal penalties. Most retirement accounts charge 10% if you take money out before 59½.
  • Bad timing. A big market drop right after you retire hurts more than one later on. Being flexible with spending helps.

Don't forget prices go up

The numbers above are in today's dollars. Prices rise over time. This is called inflation. If prices rise 3% a year, a $40,000 life today will cost about $72,000 a year in 20 years.

That sounds scary, but your investments also grow faster than inflation over long periods. A simple fix is to plan with a return that is already "after inflation." Many planners use about 5% after inflation for a stock-heavy mix. If you plan that way, you can keep your Freedom number in today's dollars.

The early-retiree budget items people miss

  • Health insurance. Medicare starts at 65. Before that, you may buy a plan on your own. Look up prices for your age and state before you set your number.
  • Big one-time costs. A new roof, a car or helping a child with college. Many people add a few thousand dollars a year to their spending for these.
  • Taxes. Money from a traditional 401(k) is taxed when you take it out. A Roth or a regular brokerage account can lower that bill. See Roth vs 401(k) vs brokerage.
  • A bad first few years. If the market drops right after you retire, selling stocks to pay bills hurts more. A year or two of spending in cash or bonds can help you wait it out.

A worked example, start to finish

Sam is 30 and spends $45,000 a year. Here is how Sam would use this guide:

  1. Spending: $45,000 a year, plus $3,000 for surprise costs. Total: $48,000.
  2. Times 25: $48,000 × 25 = $1,200,000.
  3. Retiring early: Sam wants to stop at 55, so Sam uses the safer 3.5% rule instead. $48,000 ÷ 0.035 = about $1,371,000.
  4. Other income: Sam won't count Social Security yet, to stay on the safe side.
  5. Plan: Sam has 25 years. At 7% a year, that's about $1,750 a month. Sam can start lower and raise it with each pay raise.

Sam doesn't need to hit the exact number on day one. The point is to have a target, check it once a year, and adjust.

Quick answers

How much do I need to retire at 40?

A common target is 28 to 30 times your yearly spending, since your money may need to last 50 years. For $40,000 a year, that's about $1.1 to $1.2 million.

Is the 4% rule still safe?

It held up in most past periods for 30-year retirements. It's a guide, not a guarantee. Spending a bit less in bad years makes it much safer.

Does the 4% rule include Social Security?

No. The rule is just about your investments. Social Security and other income lower how much you need to save.

Should I count my home in my freedom number?

Usually not, unless you plan to sell it. Your freedom number should be money that can pay your bills.
