How to start investing with little money

Updated October 30, 2026 · 6 min read · By Aaron Eason

You don't need thousands of dollars to start investing. Many brokers let you start with $1 and buy pieces of shares. What matters most is starting and sticking with it.

Key points

  • $100 a month at 10% could grow to about $206,000 in 30 years.
  • Fractional shares let you buy part of a share, so any amount works.
  • Build a small emergency fund and pay off high-interest debt first.

Step 1: Get ready

  • Save a small emergency fund. Even $500 to $1,000 helps you avoid selling investments when something breaks.
  • Pay off high-interest debt. A credit card at 24% costs more than investing usually earns.

Step 2: Pick an account

  • 401(k) at work: if your job matches, put in at least enough to get the full match.
  • Roth IRA: great for beginners. You pay tax now, and growth can be tax-free later.
  • Regular brokerage account: no limits and you can take money out any time.

Step 3: Pick something simple

A low-cost index fund is a popular first choice. One fund can own hundreds or thousands of companies. Examples include S&P 500 funds like VOO or FXAIX, or total market funds like VTI.

Look for a low yearly fee, under 0.10% if you can.

Step 4: Make it automatic

Set up an automatic deposit on payday. You won't miss money you never see. Turn on dividend reinvesting too.

Beginner mistakes to avoid

  • Panic selling. Markets drop sometimes. Selling in a dip locks in the loss.
  • Chasing hot tips. A boring index fund beats most stock picks over time.
  • Waiting for the "right time." Time in the market usually beats timing the market.
  • High fees. A 1% fee can cost hundreds of thousands over a lifetime.

Your first week, day by day

  1. Day 1: Check that you have some emergency cash and no high-interest credit card debt.
  2. Day 2: If your job has a 401(k) with a match, sign up for at least the match.
  3. Day 3: Open a Roth IRA or a regular brokerage account at a big, low-cost broker. It usually takes 15 minutes.
  4. Day 4: Link your bank account and move in your first amount, even if it's $25.
  5. Day 5: Buy one broad index fund, like an S&P 500 or total market fund. Fractional shares let you buy a slice for a few dollars.
  6. Day 6: Turn on automatic deposits and automatic investing for each payday.
  7. Day 7: Turn on dividend reinvesting, then close the app. You're done.

What are fractional shares?

Some funds cost hundreds of dollars for one share. Fractional shares let you buy a piece of a share instead. With $25, you might buy 0.04 of a share. Your piece grows and shrinks just like a full share, and you get your part of the dividends.

Most big brokers offer fractional shares for popular ETFs. If yours doesn't, look for a mutual fund with no minimum, or a broker that does.

How to keep going when the market drops

Your first big drop will feel bad. You may see your account fall 10% or 20%. Here's what helps:

  • Remember that you're buying shares on sale with every deposit.
  • Look at your account less often. Once a month or once a quarter is plenty.
  • Think in decades. A drop this year matters little for money you'll use in 30 years.

Common questions from beginners

Is it safe to keep my money at an online broker?

Large U.S. brokers are members of SIPC. If a broker fails, SIPC protects up to $500,000 of your securities and cash in each account type. That doesn't protect you from the market going down, but it does protect you if the company itself goes under.

Do I have to pay taxes on small investments?

In a regular brokerage account, dividends and gains you sell are taxed. In a Roth IRA, growth is tax-free if you follow the rules. That's why many beginners start with a Roth IRA when they can.

How do I know which fund to pick?

Keep it simple. One broad, low-fee index fund is enough for most beginners. You can compare funds side by side to see fees, dividends and past returns before you choose.

Quick answers

Can I start investing with $100?

Yes. Many brokers have no minimum and offer fractional shares, so you can invest $100 or even less.

What should a beginner invest in first?

Many beginners start with a low-cost, broad index fund, like an S&P 500 or total stock market fund.

Is it better to save or invest?

Do both. Keep an emergency fund in savings. Invest money you won't need for at least five years.

How much should I invest each month?

Start with any amount you can stick with. Many people aim for 10% to 15% of their pay over time.
