What is compound interest? A simple explanation with examples

Updated October 30, 2026 · 5 min read · By Aaron Eason

Compound interest is when your money earns money, and then that new money earns money too. It's the main reason small amounts can grow into big amounts over time.

Key points

  • With compound interest, you earn returns on your returns.
  • Time matters more than almost anything else.
  • $10,000 growing 8% a year becomes about $100,000 in 30 years.

A simple example

You put $1,000 in an account that grows 10% a year.

  • Year 1: you earn $100. Now you have $1,100.
  • Year 2: you earn 10% of $1,100, which is $110. Now you have $1,210.
  • Year 3: you earn $121. Now you have $1,331.

You didn't add any money. But each year's gain got bigger, because it was based on a bigger number.

Simple interest vs compound interest

With simple interest, you only earn on the money you first put in. With compound interest, you also earn on your past gains. Here's $10,000 at 8% for 30 years:

TypeAfter 30 years
Simple interest$34,000
Compound interest$100,627

Same money, same rate. Compounding ends up with almost three times as much.

Where you get compound growth

  • Savings accounts and CDs: interest is added to your balance, then earns interest too.
  • Index funds and stocks: growth and reinvested dividends build on each other. This isn't a fixed rate like a bank, and it can go down in some years.
  • Debt: compounding works against you on credit cards. Unpaid interest gets added to what you owe.

How to make compounding work for you

  1. Start early. Even small amounts get decades to grow.
  2. Add money often. Each paycheck gives compounding more to work with.
  3. Reinvest dividends. Let your earnings buy more shares.
  4. Keep fees low. Fees shrink your growth every year.
  5. Be patient. The biggest gains come near the end.

The penny that doubles

Here's a classic puzzle. Would you rather have $1 million today, or a penny that doubles every day for 30 days? Most people take the million. But the penny wins by a lot.

On day 10, the penny is only worth $5.12. On day 20, it's about $5,243. Then it takes off. By day 30, it's worth about $5.4 million. Half of all that money shows up on the very last day.

Real investments don't double every day. But the shape is the same. Compounding looks slow at first, then speeds up. That's why the last years of investing often add more than the first decades combined.

The early starter vs the late starter

Meet two savers. Both earn 7% a year.

  • Ben invests $5,000 a year from age 25 to 35. Then he stops for good. He put in $50,000 total.
  • Amy waits until 35. She invests $5,000 a year from 35 to 65, every year for 30 years. She put in $150,000 total.
SaverYears investingTotal put inAt age 65
Ben (early)10$50,000$525,872

Ben put in one third as much money, yet he ends up with more. His money had 10 extra years to compound. That's the power of time.

Of course, the best plan is to be both: start early and keep going. If Ben had kept investing until 65, he'd have about $1 million.

Compounding can work against you, too

Credit card debt compounds the same way, just in the wrong direction. Many cards charge over 20% a year. A $3,000 balance at 22% that you never pay down would grow to more than $8,000 in 5 years. That's why paying off high-interest debt is often the best "investment" you can make.

Three numbers that control compounding

  1. Time: how many years your money grows. This matters most.
  2. Rate: how fast it grows each year. Fees and taxes lower this, so keep them small.
  3. Deposits: how much you add along the way. Steady deposits give compounding more to work with.

You can't control the market's returns. But you can control when you start, how much you add, and how much you pay in fees.

APY vs APR: the label that tells you about compounding

Banks show two kinds of rates. APR is the simple yearly rate. APY includes the effect of compounding during the year. If interest is added every month, a 5% APR works out to about a 5.12% APY, because each month's interest earns interest of its own.

When you're earning, like in a savings account, compare APYs. When you're borrowing, like on a car loan or a card, look closely at the APR and how often interest is added. The same compounding that helps savers makes debt grow faster.

A quick way to estimate: the rule of 72

Want to know how long it takes your money to double? Divide 72 by the yearly return. At 8%, money doubles about every 9 years. At 6%, about every 12 years. It's a handy shortcut you can do in your head. Read more in our rule of 72 guide.

Quick answers

What is compound interest in simple words?

It's earning money on your money and also on the money it already earned. Your gains start making their own gains.

How often does interest compound?

It depends. Many savings accounts compound daily or monthly. With investments, growth builds whenever prices rise and dividends are reinvested.

Is compound interest good or bad?

It's good when you're saving or investing. It's bad when you owe money, like on a credit card, because your debt grows faster.

How long does it take to double my money?

Divide 72 by your yearly rate. At 8%, money doubles in about 9 years. This is called the Rule of 72.
