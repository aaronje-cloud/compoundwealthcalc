High-yield savings account vs CD: which should you pick in 2026?

Updated October 30, 2026 · 5 min read · By Aaron Eason

Both are safe places for money you'll need soon. The big difference: a savings account lets you take money out any time, while a CD locks your money and your rate.

Key points

  • As of October 7, 2026, a typical high-yield savings rate was about 3.50%. Top 1-year CDs paid about 4.75%.
  • The average savings account paid only 0.37%.
  • Both are protected up to $250,000 at FDIC-insured banks.

How each one works

High-yield savings: you can add or take out money whenever you want. The bank can raise or lower the rate at any time.

CD (certificate of deposit): you put in one amount for a set time, like 1 year. Your rate is locked. If you take it out early, you usually lose a few months of interest.

When to use a high-yield savings account

  • Your emergency fund. Many people keep 3 to 6 months of bills here.
  • Money you're adding to often.
  • Money you might need on short notice.

When to use a CD

  • Money for a set date, like a car or a home down payment in 1 to 3 years.
  • When you think rates might fall and want to lock today's rate.
  • When you want a rule that stops you from spending the money.

Try a CD ladder

A CD ladder splits your money across CDs that end at different times. For example, put $2,000 each into 1-, 2-, 3-, 4- and 5-year CDs. Each year, one CD ends. You can use that money or roll it into a new 5-year CD. You get locked rates, but some money is free every year.

Are they better than investing?

For short-term money, yes. They don't drop in value like stocks can. For goals more than 5 years away, like retirement, investing has usually grown much more. Savings rates often only keep up with rising prices.

Say you have $10,000 you won't need for a while, but you might need some of it in a year or two. A ladder splits it up:

Rung 1$2,5001 year
Rung 2$2,5002 years

Rung 4$2,5004 years

Each year, one CD finishes. If you don't need the money, you put it into a new 4-year CD. After a few years, every rung is a 4-year CD, which often pays the most, but one of them still finishes every single year. You get higher rates and regular access to your money.

What breaking a CD early costs

Most banks charge a penalty if you take money out of a CD before it ends. A common penalty on a 1-year CD is three months of interest. On a $10,000 CD paying 4%, that's about $100. Longer CDs often charge more, like six or twelve months of interest.

Read the penalty before you open a CD. Some banks offer "no-penalty" CDs. They usually pay a little less, but you can take your money out early for free.

Is my money safe?

At a bank insured by the FDIC, or a credit union insured by the NCUA, your deposits are protected up to $250,000 per person, per bank, for each type of account. That covers both savings accounts and CDs. If you have more than that, you can spread it across different banks.

Before you open an account at an online bank you haven't heard of, check that it's FDIC-insured. You can look it up on the FDIC's website.

How rates change

High-yield savings rates can change any day. When the Federal Reserve lowers interest rates, savings rates usually drop soon after. A CD locks your rate for the whole term, so it can be a smart move when rates are expected to fall.

When rates are rising, a high-yield savings account lets you benefit right away. Many people keep their emergency fund in high-yield savings and put money for a known future goal, like a house down payment in two years, into a CD.

A simple plan for most people

  1. Emergency fund: three to six months of bills in a high-yield savings account. You can get it any day.
  2. Known goals in 1 to 5 years: a car, a wedding or a down payment. A CD or a CD ladder that ends around the date you need it.
  3. Goals 5 or more years away: retirement and other long-term goals usually belong in investments like index funds, which have grown faster over long periods.

Keeping each pot of money in the right place means you're never forced to sell investments in a bad year just to pay a surprise bill. See how much should be in an emergency fund.

Quick answers

Is a CD better than a high-yield savings account?

A CD is better if you won't need the money and want a locked rate. A high-yield savings account is better if you might need the money or are still adding to it.

Can you lose money in a CD?

Not at an FDIC-insured bank, up to $250,000. But if you take money out early, you can lose some interest.

Is interest from savings and CDs taxed?

Yes. It's taxed like regular income in the year you earn it. Banks send a 1099-INT form if you earn $10 or more.

How much should I keep in savings?

Many people keep 3 to 6 months of bills in an emergency fund. Money for long-term goals usually goes into investments.
