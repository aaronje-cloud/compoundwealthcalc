VOO vs SPY: which is better for you?

Updated October 30, 2026 · 5 min read · By Aaron Eason

VOO and SPY both follow the S&P 500, so they own the same companies. The real differences are cost and who they're built for.

The short answer

For most long-term investors, VOO is the better pick because it costs less (0.03% vs about 0.09%). SPY is mainly favored by traders because it trades the most shares each day.

What is VOO?

VOO follows the S&P 500, a list of about 500 of the biggest U.S. companies across every industry. It's one of the cheapest and most popular ways to own the U.S. stock market.

What is SPY?

SPY started in 1993 and was the first U.S. ETF. It follows the same S&P 500 list as VOO. It trades more shares each day than almost any fund, which is why short-term traders love it.

Risk

VOO's typical swings are medium, and SPY's are medium. Funds that hold fewer kinds of companies tend to rise and fall more. The best fund is one you can hold through a bad year without selling.

Which one fits you?

  • Pick VOO if you're investing for the long run and want the lowest fee.
  • Pick SPY if you trade often or use options, where SPY's huge trading volume helps.

A little history

SPY started in January 1993. It was the first ETF in the United States, and it's still one of the most traded investments on Earth. VOO came along in 2010 from Vanguard, which is known for low fees. Both own the same S&P 500 companies in the same amounts.

SPY is set up as a unit investment trust. That's an older type of fund with stricter rules. For example, it holds dividends as cash until it pays them out each quarter, instead of putting them back to work right away. VOO is a regular open-end fund, so it can reinvest that cash in the meantime. Over long periods, this is a tiny edge for VOO.

Who should pick SPY?

SPY's superpower is trading. So many shares change hands every second that big investors can buy or sell millions of dollars of it with almost no price impact. Its options market is one of the busiest in the world. If you're an active trader or you use options, those things can matter more than a small fee.

If you're putting part of each paycheck into a fund and holding for years, none of that helps you. You'd just pay the higher fee.

I already own SPY. Should I switch to VOO?

  • In a retirement account (401(k), IRA, Roth IRA): you can switch without taxes.
  • In a regular brokerage account: think twice. If you own $30,000 of SPY that you bought for $18,000, selling means a $12,000 gain. At 15%, that's about $1,800 in tax. The lower fee saves about $18 a year on $30,000. Keeping your SPY and sending new money to VOO is usually smarter.

Other cheap S&P 500 choices

VOO isn't the only low-cost option. iShares' IVV also charges 0.03%. Fidelity's FXAIX mutual fund charges 0.015%. See VOO vs FXAIX. The most important thing isn't which S&P 500 fund you pick. It's that you keep buying it, every payday, for a long time.

Quick answers

Is VOO better than SPY?

For most long-term investors, VOO is the better pick because it costs less (0.03% vs about 0.09%). SPY is mainly favored by traders because it trades the most shares each day.

Which has lower fees, VOO or SPY?

VOO charges 0.03% a year and SPY charges 0.09%. VOO is cheaper.

Which pays more dividends, VOO or SPY?

VOO has paid about 1.3% a year and SPY about 1.2%. Yields change over time.

Do VOO and SPY own the same stocks?

Yes. Both follow the S&P 500, so they hold the same companies in almost the same amounts.
