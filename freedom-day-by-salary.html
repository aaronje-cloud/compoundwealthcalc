Freedom Day by salary: how many years until you can stop working?

Updated October 30, 2026 · 8 min read · By Aaron Eason

Your Freedom Day is the day your investments could pay for your life, so work becomes a choice. Most people guess that a bigger salary gets you there faster. That's only half true. The real driver is how much of your pay you keep.

Key points

  • Your savings rate (the share of take-home pay you invest) decides how many years it takes.
  • At a 15% savings rate, it takes about 43 years. At 25%, about 32. At 50%, about 17.
  • A bigger salary helps most when your lifestyle doesn't grow with it.
  • All examples use 5% growth a year after inflation and the 4% rule, starting from $0.

How we did the math

Every example follows the same simple steps:

  1. Start with your take-home pay, the money that reaches your bank after taxes.
  2. Some of it gets invested. The rest is what you live on.
  3. Your Freedom number is 25 times what you live on each year. That's the 4% rule.
  4. Your investments grow 5% a year after inflation, and you add the same amount every year until you reach the number.

Because we use a return that's already "after inflation," every dollar amount on this page is in today's dollars.

Notice how the years fall quickly at first. Going from 10% to 20% cuts 15 years. Going from 20% to 30% cuts 9 more. Each extra bit you save works two ways: more money is growing, and you need a smaller Freedom number because you've learned to live on less.

The jump from 5% to 10% is worth noticing too. It takes 14 years off. If you can't save much right now, even a small bump makes a real difference.

This is good news if you don't earn a lot. A teacher who invests 25% reaches Freedom Day at the same time as a doctor who invests 25%.

When salary does matter: keep your lifestyle the same

Salary starts to matter when your spending stays put while your pay goes up. Here, everyone lives on $50,000 a year, so everyone's Freedom number is $1,250,000. Whatever they earn above $50,000 gets invested.

Example: Maya, take-home pay of $60,000

Maya is 28 and brings home $60,000 a year. She invests 15%, or $9,000. At that pace, she'd reach Freedom Day in about 43 years, at 71.

Maya makes three changes over the next few years:

  • She gets her full 401(k) match, which adds $1,800 a year she wasn't counting.
  • She moves to a cheaper apartment and saves $250 a month.
  • She sends half of each raise to her investments.

Within a few years she's investing about 30% of her pay. Her Freedom Day moves from about 43 years away to about 28 years away. That's roughly 15 years of her life back, and her salary barely changed.

Things that can move your Freedom Day sooner

  • Money you've already saved. These tables start at $0. If you have savings now, you're ahead.
  • Employer matches. Free money adds to your savings rate without lowering what you live on.
  • Social Security or a pension. Income you'll get later means you need a smaller pile. See Coast FIRE for a related idea.
  • Lower fees. A 1% fee can add years. See do ETF fees matter?

What about my 401(k)?

If money goes into a 401(k) before taxes, it doesn't show up in your take-home pay. To find your true savings rate, add your 401(k) deposits and any employer match to both what you save and what you take home. For example, if you take home $50,000, invest $5,000 in a Roth IRA and also put $6,000 into a 401(k) with a $3,000 match, your savings rate is $14,000 ÷ $59,000, or about 24%.

Things that can push it later

  • Lower returns. If your money grows 4% after inflation instead of 5%, every row takes a few years longer.
  • Lifestyle creep. Spending more every time you earn more keeps your savings rate stuck.
  • Selling in a crash. The tables assume you keep investing through good years and bad ones.
  • Health insurance before 65. If you stop working early, plan for this cost.

Quick answers

Does a higher salary mean I can retire sooner?

Only if you invest more of it. If your spending grows as fast as your pay, your Freedom Day stays about the same. What really counts is your savings rate.

What savings rate do I need to retire early?

In our examples, investing about 25% of take-home pay takes about 32 years, and 50% takes about 17 years, starting from $0 with 5% growth after inflation.

Should I use take-home pay or gross salary?

We use take-home pay because that's the money you actually decide how to spend. If you save in a 401(k) before taxes, add that amount to both your savings and your take-home pay.

Why use 5% instead of 10%?

Stocks have averaged around 10% a year over the long run, but about 3% of that has been lost to inflation. Using 5% after inflation is a careful middle ground and keeps every number in today's dollars.
